Golfo Dulce is a roughly 200 m deep basin cut into the southern Pacific coast of Costa Rica, connected to the open ocean by an entrance shallow enough to choke off free exchange with the sea outside. That combination — a deep, sheltered basin behind a shallow threshold — is the same physical recipe that makes high-latitude fjords go anoxic at depth, except this one sits almost on the equator, in year-round tropical heat and rainfall [@hebbeln-1996-morphology]. It is one of only four tropical fjords known anywhere in the world [@marin-vindas-2025-bacterioplancton], and the water trapped below its sill is, at times, one of the most oxygen-starved marine environments on the planet's surface.

## What "anoxic" means here, and why "permanently" is the wrong word

Anoxic water has no measurable dissolved oxygen at all — a step beyond merely hypoxic (low-oxygen) water. Golfo Dulce's deep basin has repeatedly been found in that state: oxygen near zero below the pycnocline (the depth band where density changes sharply, discussed below), nitrate being consumed by bacteria that use it instead of oxygen to respire, and traces of toxic hydrogen sulfide near the bottom [@richards-1971-anoxic-basin]. It is tempting to describe the gulf as permanently anoxic. The evidence does not support that.

Eight cruises between June 1993 and September 1995 at the deep 200 m station found deep oxygen persistently low — between 3.00 and 0.20 mg/L — but never actually reaching zero [@acuna-gonzalez-2006-snapshot]. Separate surveys from 2005–2007 found the same pattern at 70–140 m: measurably low oxygen (0.25–1.10 mg/L) but not absent, though that survey did not reach the basin floor [@morales-ramirez-2015-oceanografia]. The most current synthesis of the literature — 123 studies reviewed together — settles on "frequently anoxic ... with occasional formation of H2S," not "always" [@vargas-zamora-2021-fiordo]. The mechanism behind the intermittency has been worked out directly: frequent inflows of oxygenated water from the Pacific keep sulfide from accumulating, holding it below about 7 micromoles per litre in the deepest ~100 m even though that layer is genuinely without oxygen much of the time [@ferdelman-2006-oxygen-nitrogen-sulfur]. The basin cycles between anoxic and renewed states rather than sitting fixed in either one — closer to the Black Sea's periodic renewal than to a basin that is chemically dead year-round. A survey that re-occupied the exact stations sampled 25 years earlier found essentially the same vertical zonation reappearing, read by its authors as evidence that exchange happens often enough to prevent the basin from ever settling into a permanently reducing state [@thamdrup-1996-biogeochemical-survey].

Worth being honest about: the public oxygen record for this basin is close to frozen at 1969–1970 measurements [@richards-1971-anoxic-basin], and whether today's structure still matches that record is an open question, covered in full on the [gap register](/en/gaps).

## Water column structure

The water column is layered, and the layering is what makes anoxia possible at all. A thermocline — a band, roughly 25 to 60 m deep, across which temperature drops sharply — is closely tracked by a halocline (a comparable jump in salinity) around 40 m, separating a lighter surface layer near salinity 34 from a thicker, denser bottom layer near salinity 35 [@acuna-gonzalez-2006-snapshot]. Where temperature and salinity both change sharply together, so does density, producing a pycnocline that acts as a lid, suppressing vertical mixing and cutting the water beneath it off from atmospheric oxygen. Oxygen tends to persist to roughly 100 m before falling away, with a bacterial population maximum forming where oxygen itself drops below about 1 micromolar [@ferdelman-2006-oxygen-nitrogen-sulfur]. Below that, nitrogen chemistry takes over as the dominant respiratory pathway, and dissolved inorganic nitrogen is measurably depleted beneath the pycnocline, at a deficit around 2.9 mol per square metre of seafloor [@ferdelman-2006-oxygen-nitrogen-sulfur].

Because the gulf sits so close to the equator, the Coriolis effect — the rotational force that organises circulation in most fjords at higher latitudes — is negligible here. Cross-gulf structure is instead set by river runoff, local wind and basin shape, while along-gulf temperature and salinity gradients are maintained by the inner rivers [@svendsen-2006-physical-oceanography]. The vertical structure also differs by location: a three-layer flow dominates near the sill, while the inner basin shows a simpler two-layer estuarine circulation sitting above slowly moving, largely stagnant deep water [@svendsen-2006-physical-oceanography].

## The sill, and how well-constrained its depth really is

None of this works without the sill — the shallow ridge near the gulf's entrance that restricts how much Pacific water reaches the inner basin. Its existence is not in dispute: echosounder surveys from 1994 describe a fjord-like structure with a deep inner basin, over 200 m, sheltered from the open Pacific by a shallow sill [@hebbeln-1996-morphology]. How shallow is genuinely unsettled. Field campaigns paired with a circulation model give an "effective sill depth" of about 60 m, inferred from how restricted exchange actually is [@svendsen-2006-sill]. A 2009–2010 CTD campaign instead states the sill is shallower than 30 m, citing Richards' original 1971 work — and both figures appear within two sentences of each other in that same paper, unreconciled [@rincon-2016-hidrografia]. Neither number traces to a survey line actually run across the sill: the ~60 m figure is inferred rather than measured, and the CTD campaign quoting a sill depth under 30 m only reached 66 m on its own casts [@rincon-2016-hidrografia] [@svendsen-2006-sill]. The sill is shallow enough to strongly restrict exchange and deep enough that Pacific water gets in episodically — but its precise depth, a two-fold discrepancy between competing estimates, has never been resolved by direct survey. The inner basin itself is generally given as reaching about 200–215 m at its deepest point [@svendsen-2006-sill] [@hebbeln-1996-morphology], though, as detailed on the gap register, the bathymetric coverage backing that figure is extremely sparse.

## Freshwater input

Golfo Dulce is fed by rain and rivers on a scale that shapes almost everything about it: catchment precipitation exceeds 4.5 m a year [@allencoralatlas-2026-bathymetry]. Three river systems dominate the freshwater budget: the Esquinas and Coto Colorado, which together carry the great majority of agricultural drainage — the Coto Colorado sub-basin alone accounts for about 95 percent of the agricultural area draining into the gulf [@fournier-2019-agroquimicos] — and the Rincón, further along the Osa side [@umana-1998-drainage-rivers]. A baseline survey of these rivers found dissolved oxygen consistently well-oxygenated at every site, while suspended-solid loads varied enormously with discharge — the variable governing how much sediment and turbidity actually reaches the gulf [@umana-1998-drainage-rivers]. That control has since been quantified from thirty-three years of satellite imagery: a 15 m strip of intact riparian vegetation measurably reduces turbidity out to 800 m offshore, while pasture and gravel roads increase it, with the effect strongest on short, steep, low-discharge streams [@brumberg-2026-turbidity].

Freshwater influence is not limited to the open gulf. Golfito Bay's own mangrove estuaries, sampled monthly through 1998, are vertically well-mixed rather than stratified, and carry very high silicate — up to roughly 980 micromoles per litre in the rainy season — with strongly seasonal nitrate, and no anoxic conditions [@silva-2006-estuarios-golfito]. The shallow, land-facing estuarine fringe behaves completely differently from the deep, stratified inner basin close by.

## Tides, circulation, and renewal

Circulation is generally sluggish and set by runoff, wind and topography, but not static. A monthly time series spanning the strong 1997–1998 El Niño found twice-yearly intrusions of cold, saline, oxygen-rich Pacific water pushing into the basin, tentatively linked to the same forcing that drives coastal upwelling in the nearby Gulf of Panama [@quesada-alpizar-2004-masas-de-agua] — giving the renewal process, which periodically breaks the anoxic cycle, a rough seasonal rhythm and a plausible regional driver. Earlier work had already caught such an event directly: two hydrographic surveys eight days apart in March 1969 documented an active influx of ocean water, leading Richards and colleagues to conclude the basin receives ocean water erratically, in winter and spring, rather than staying permanently stagnant [@richards-1971-anoxic-basin]. Surface currents near the sill run around 18 cm/s, decaying to roughly 5 cm/s toward the interior [@morales-ramirez-2015-oceanografia]. Salt-balance calculations put the deep basin's water residence time at 35 ± 57 days — a genuinely wide range, order-of-magnitude at best, but implying renewal on a timescale of weeks to a couple of months rather than years [@ferdelman-2006-oxygen-nitrogen-sulfur].

## The nitrogen cycle and anammox

The single reason Golfo Dulce matters to ocean science well beyond Costa Rica is anammox — anaerobic ammonium oxidation, a reaction in which bacteria combine ammonium and nitrite directly to produce nitrogen gas without needing oxygen. Before the early 2000s, anammox was known mainly from engineered wastewater reactors and marine sediments. Using isotope-labelling in the gulf's anoxic water column, researchers showed for the first time that anammox also runs in open water, accounting for 19–35 percent of all nitrogen gas production there [@dalsgaard-2003-anammox]. That mattered globally because Golfo Dulce's chemistry closely resembles the much larger oxygen minimum zones of the open ocean, where a third to a half of all fixed nitrogen is thought to be permanently removed from the marine nitrogen budget — this small Costa Rican basin became a natural laboratory proving anammox a major, previously underappreciated term in that global budget [@dalsgaard-2003-anammox].

The nitrogen story extends beyond the open water column. Sinking carcasses of the gulf's abundant copepods and ostracods carry their own anoxic microenvironments as they fall, hosting active anammox, denitrification and related nitrogen-loss reactions even while surrounded by oxygenated water [@stief-2017-carcasas]. Sulfide never simply builds up despite active sulfate reduction because sulfide-oxidising bacteria — free-swimming forms and mat-forming Beggiatoa — sit at the sediment-water interface and in the bottom water, consuming it almost as fast as it forms [@kuever-1996-microbiological]. A methane system sits alongside: bottom water can hold methane up to 1.7 micromoles per litre, yet a microbial community anchored just below the oxic–anoxic boundary oxidises it anaerobically fast enough to act as a filter limiting escape upward [@steinsdottir-2022-methane-oxidation]. Shallow cold methane seeps, unrelated to this deep anoxic chemistry, also vent at around 10 m depth elsewhere in the gulf — shallow warmth and cold seepage together, a combination previously unknown outside hydrothermal settings [@wild-2015-methane-seep] [@salas-2023-gas-seeps].

Together, this is what makes Golfo Dulce more than a scenic curiosity: a small, accessible, intermittently anoxic basin whose nitrogen chemistry mirrors processes that, at ocean-basin scale, help set the size of the global marine nitrogen budget [@dalsgaard-2003-anammox] — with a physical trigger, the sill, whose exact depth is still unsettled [@rincon-2016-hidrografia], and an oxygen record barely checked since 1970.
