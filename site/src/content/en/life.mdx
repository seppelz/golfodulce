Golfo Dulce packs a lot of life into a small basin. A 2010 synthesis compiled 1,028 marine species recorded from the gulf — 21.5% of everything reported for the whole Costa Rican Pacific coast — with more than double the per-km² species density of the larger Golfo de Nicoya [@morales-ramirez-2010-amenazas]. What makes the gulf distinctive is how that life is organized vertically: below roughly 50 m the water loses oxygen, and below about 150–200 m the inner basin is frequently anoxic, with occasional hydrogen sulfide [@vargas-zamora-2021-fiordo]. Everything below should be read against that structure — biodiverse near the surface, increasingly exclusionary with depth.

This project's own analysis of OBIS and GBIF occurrence data — 17,188 deduplicated records spanning 1896–2026 across roughly 1,952 species — makes the shape of the public record concrete. Only 12.1% carry a usable depth value once a data-quality problem in one GBIF collection is corrected for (that dataset copies land elevation into the depth field for preserved specimens, which would otherwise make the record look far better sampled at depth than it is). Of the records with a genuine depth, 68 sit at or below 100 m and 32 below 200 m, with the deepest at 500 m. The most frequently recorded taxa are coastal generalists — hermit crab, pelican, crocodile, shorebirds — not deep-water specialists, reflecting what has been observed rather than what actually lives in the gulf.

## Humpback whales from two hemispheres

Golfo Dulce hosts humpback whales from both Northern and Southern Hemisphere breeding populations, in the same waters at different times of year, first documented by early-1990s surveys [@acevedo-1995-overlap]. Photo-identification later matched seven individuals between Antarctic feeding grounds and Central American wintering waters — an 8,300 km migration, the longest recorded for any mammal — confirming Southern Hemisphere animals occupy waters a Northern Hemisphere population uses about six months apart, apparently because a cold South Pacific water tongue pushes the southern range abnormally far north [@rasmussen-2007-migracion]. Most humpback grounds belong to one population per season; here, whales from opposite ends of the ocean share a bay months apart. Surveys through the 2010s show the Southern Hemisphere population partitioning the space: mother-calf pairs stay shallower than 80 m, resting and nursing, while adults use deeper water [@pelayo-gonzalez-2022-habitat] — nursery use that lines up with where boat traffic concentrates.

## Dolphins and sea turtles

The resident cetacean community follows the same inner-basin-to-mouth gradient that governs the gulf's hydrography: bottlenose and pantropical spotted dolphins dominate the inner basin, bottlenose and migratory humpbacks concentrate at the sill, and the most diverse, oceanic community occupies the mouth [@oviedo-2015-cetaceos]. Bottlenose foraging tracks river mouths, reefs and mangrove edges almost exclusively, and animals rarely cross the gulf [@cubero-pardo-2007-delfines]. A resident core of roughly 187–368 spotted dolphins uses the inner basin as critical foraging habitat, alongside many more seen only once [@oviedo-2023-stenella]. Rarer species: false killer whales, fewer than 100 individuals [@sanchez-robledo-2020-pseudorca], and whale sharks, feeding near river mouths on zooplankton fueled by river nutrients [@pacheco-polanco-2015-tiburonballena], though their presence is strongly pulsed — one field survey recorded zero sightings against a local-knowledge estimate of up to 226 individuals [@hanna-2016-rhincodon].

Two turtle species use the gulf as a foraging ground rather than a nesting site: the critically endangered hawksbill, dominated by juveniles [@chacon-chaverri-2015-carey], and the green turtle, one recorded feeder originally tagged in the Galápagos [@chacon-chaverri-2015b-verde]. A broad baseline survey confirmed animals use the gulf for foraging, mating and resting rather than merely passing through [@bessesen-2015-vertebrados], and a decade-apart test of fisher and guide knowledge against independent surveys found it a useful complement to direct observation [@bessesen-2021-lek].

## Mangroves: the gulf's own stands, and Térraba-Sierpe to the north

The gulf's own mangrove stands are structurally distinct from each other: Rhizophora racemosa and Pelliciera rhizophorae dominate all three surveyed, but canopy height, density and composition vary sharply enough that "the Golfo Dulce mangrove" is not one thing [@samper-villarreal-2015-manglares]. They support a working fishery and nursery — piangua cockle density varied twentyfold between two sites, mean shell length already below the legal minimum where harvest is heaviest, growth overfishing in progress [@silva-benavides-2015-anadara] — and the only dedicated fish survey of a Golfo Dulce mangrove recorded 82 species, more than half estuarine transients, 71% commercially valuable [@feutry-2010-zancudo]. North of the Osa Peninsula, the Térraba-Sierpe National Wetland, Costa Rica's largest mangrove system, spans roughly 14,800 hectares — an order of magnitude larger, delta-built rather than embayment-sheltered, but sharing the same core species pool [@acuna-piedra-2018-terraba].

## Coral and reef presence

Reefs persist inside this sediment-loaded basin, though not as coral-dominated systems. A resurvey of 27 sites across the wider Osa Conservation Area found turf algae dominant (62.7% cover) and live coral cover moderate (16.5%), but recorded coral recovery at Golfo Dulce and Caño Island, and the highest macroinvertebrate diversity of any locality surveyed [@alvarado-2015-acosa]. A census at the La Viuda islet recorded 28 reef-fish species, diversity tracking season and wave exposure [@palacios-martinez-2015-viuda]. Sediment cores from the Punta Islotes fringing reef show at least 9 m of accumulation beginning around 5,500 years ago, growth collapsing recently, attributed to sediment from catchment deforestation [@cortes-1991-arrecifes]. Interviews with fishers and tourism operators found over 60% independently naming sedimentation and agrochemical contamination as the principal threat, matching biophysical surveys directly [@villalobos-cubero-2023-arrecifes]. The gulf also holds a shallow cold methane seep at 10 m, venting through a carbonate platform in fully oxygenated water — previously known only from deep hydrothermal settings — hosting a fish and cryptofauna community unlike anything else on the coast [@wild-2015-methane-seep; @salas-2023-gas-seeps].

## Fisheries

Golfo Dulce's artisanal fisheries are shaped as much by the gulf's oxygen structure as by fish behavior, because the deeper bottom is largely unfishable. Ray-fishing operations concentrate over muddy bottoms shallower than 30 m across named grounds — where the demersal fishery can physically operate above a floor that turns hypoxic to anoxic below [@lopez-garro-2015b-dasyatis]. Sharks and rays made up more than half the catch across 67 monitored bottom-longline trips, scalloped hammerhead alone just over half of all sharks, nearly all juveniles [@lopez-garro-2016-tiburones]; a focused study found all sampled hammerheads were juveniles, smallest animals and highest catch rates coinciding in a July-August pulse consistent with a pupping season, prompting a proposed seasonal closure where catches concentrate [@zanella-2016-martillo]. The same boundary shapes fish diversity: of 19 flatfish species recorded in the gulf, 58% were taken below 100 m and probably exposed to hypoxia [@vargas-2020-flatfish].

## How the oxygen structure shapes everything below the surface

This is the gulf's defining feature, and it needs careful framing. The gulf is not permanently anoxic — the deep basin is frequently, not continuously, anoxic, with the boundary shifting with sill-overflow events and season [@vargas-zamora-2021-fiordo]. The ecological record shows not a dead zone but a moving boundary that compresses and excludes life as it deepens.

Zooplankton is the clearest case. Sampling during the 1997–1998 El Niño found the community sharply concentrated above the thermocline at every station, with a markedly impoverished deep layer throughout [@quesada-alpizar-2006-zooplancton], and finer sampling of two ostracod species found them overwhelmingly confined to the top 20 m — only 2% and 0.4% of individuals below 50 m, earliest life stages essentially never leaving the surface [@morales-ramirez-2008-ostracodos]. That has a specific consequence: diel vertical migration, near-universal elsewhere in the ocean, is truncated here, because the water below the surface layer is not somewhere the animals can survive migrating into. Direct measurement at 70–140 m found oxygen not wholly absent but low enough, with nitrate from decomposing sinking matter, to explain the compression above it [@morales-ramirez-2015-oceanografia].

The most direct test of what lives on the basin floor comes from a single 1993 cruise: box cores across nine stations recovered 1,690 individuals in 69 species, but the two deepest stations at 200 m yielded no organisms at all, while a diverse fauna occurred at comparable depths just outside the sill [@leon-morales-1998-macroinfauna]. It would be easy to read that as proof the floor is permanently lifeless, but the same paper complicates the reading: against a 1976 survey at similar stations, species composition had turned over almost completely while diversity stayed similar, and the authors attribute both the turnover and the 1993 azoic result to fluctuating hypoxia-anoxia compounded by El Niño — not a fixed, permanent absence of life. It is a snapshot of one anoxic episode, not proof that nothing can live there when conditions differ.

One species is the strongest evidence the oxygen structure has shaped evolution, not just distribution. A yellow-bodied subspecies of pelagic sea snake, Hydrophis platurus xanthos, is restricted to roughly 320 km² of the inner basin — water the describing paper itself calls occasionally anoxic — and found nowhere else, despite the parent species ranging across the entire tropical Indo-Pacific and eastern Pacific [@bessesen-2017-xanthos]. The authors propose the shallow sill restricts gene flow with the wider oceanic population, isolating it behind a bathymetric barrier much as an island isolates land animals.

Even decay chemistry is coupled to the plankton here unusually. Labeling experiments on sinking copepod and ostracod carcasses showed they carry their own anoxic microenvironments down through the water column, hosting denitrification, ammonium production and anammox even where ambient oxygen is present [@stief-2017-carcasas]. That extends a larger finding: anaerobic ammonium oxidation, previously known mainly from sediments and engineered reactors, was first shown to matter in an open marine water column in Golfo Dulce, accounting for 19–35% of nitrogen gas production in the anoxic layer — significant enough to make this small basin a proof of concept cited across subsequent oxygen-minimum-zone research [@dalsgaard-2003-anammox]. The carcass finding shows the process is not confined to the deep water column: it travels downward attached to individual dead animals, seeding anoxic chemistry into otherwise oxygenated water.

The public occurrence record for this zone remains thin, as this project's own OBIS analysis shows — barely a few dozen depth-tagged records below 100 m in the whole dataset. That is a gap in observation, covered in more depth on this site's gap register, not a claim the deep gulf is empty. The evidence supports a consistent pattern — zooplankton, ostracods, flatfish, box-core macrofauna, an endemic sea snake, and the nitrogen chemistry of sinking carcasses — of life compressed toward the surface and excluded with depth, tracking a boundary that itself moves rather than sitting fixed at one line on a chart.
