Golfo Dulce packs an unusual amount of life into a small basin. A 2010 synthesis of the published literature compiled 1,028 marine species recorded from the gulf — 21.5% of everything reported for the whole Costa Rican Pacific coast, in a fraction of the coastline — and found the gulf carries more than double the per-km² species density of the larger Golfo de Nicoya for macrocrustaceans, molluscs, marine worms, plankton and fish [@morales-ramirez-2010-amenazas]. What makes the gulf scientifically distinctive, though, is not just how much lives here but how that life is organized vertically. Below roughly 50 m the water loses oxygen, and below about 150–200 m the inner basin is frequently anoxic, with occasional hydrogen sulfide [@vargas-zamora-2021-fiordo]. Every section below should be read against that structure: a gulf that is biodiverse at the surface and increasingly exclusionary with depth.

This project's own analysis of OBIS occurrence data — a complete pull of 6,513 records spanning 1933–2026 across 26 datasets, aggregating to roughly 687 distinct taxa — makes the shape of the public record concrete. Only 6.8% of those records carry a depth value at all, just 57 sit at or below 100 m, only 24 below 200 m, and the single deepest record is 320 m. The most frequently recorded taxa are not deep-water specialists but coastal generalists: brown pelican, American crocodile, shorebirds. That is a real pattern in what has been observed and reported, not necessarily a true picture of what lives in the gulf — a distinction worth keeping in mind through everything that follows.

## Humpback whales from two hemispheres

The single fact that puts Golfo Dulce on the map for cetacean biologists is that it hosts humpback whales from both the Northern and Southern Hemisphere breeding populations, in the same waters, at different times of year. The first published sightings — including cow-calf pairs — establishing this geographic overlap came from surveys in the early 1990s [@acevedo-1995-overlap]. It was later placed on firmer quantitative footing: photo-identification matched seven individual whales between their Antarctic feeding grounds and Central American wintering waters, documenting a roughly 8,300 km migration — the longest recorded for any mammal — and showing that these Southern Hemisphere animals occupy the same wintering waters a Northern Hemisphere population uses roughly six months apart [@rasmussen-2007-migracion]. The proposed mechanism is that a tongue of unusually cold water in the eastern South Pacific pushes the southern population's wintering range abnormally far north, so that instead of the two populations' ranges sitting in different hemispheres by season as usual, they land on the same patch of ocean.

Why this matters: most humpback populations winter in waters that are effectively theirs alone for the season. Golfo Dulce is one of the few places on Earth where a visitor could, in principle, see whales from opposite ends of the planet's ocean basins using the identical bay, simply at different months. A decade of effort-corrected surveys (2010–2019) inside the gulf itself shows how the Southern Hemisphere population, specifically, partitions the space once it arrives: mother-calf pairs stay in water shallower than 80 m, mostly resting and nursing, while adult-only groups use deeper water [@pelayo-gonzalez-2022-habitat]. That shallow-water nursery use lines up directly with where boat traffic in the gulf concentrates, which is the basis for treating whale habitat as a concrete input to vessel-management rules, not an abstraction.

## Dolphins and sea turtles

The gulf supports a resident cetacean community structured along the same inner-basin-to-mouth gradient that governs its hydrography. A ten-year survey (2005–2014, 943 sightings, over 3,490 hours and 64,533 km of effort) found coastal bottlenose and pantropical spotted dolphins dominating the inner basin, bottlenose dolphins and migratory humpbacks concentrated at the sill, and the most diverse, oceanic dolphin community in the transitional water at the gulf's mouth [@oviedo-2015-cetaceos]. Bottlenose dolphin foraging tracks specific coastal features — river mouths, coral reef patches, mangrove edges — almost exclusively, and animals rarely cross the gulf, suggesting side-fidelity within the embayment [@cubero-pardo-2007-delfines]. A capture-recapture study of pantropical spotted dolphins found a resident core of roughly 187–368 animals with very high survival, concentrated in the inner basin as a foraging ground, alongside a much larger set of individuals seen only once — an open population built around a small resident nucleus [@oviedo-2023-stenella]. Rarer species are present too: false killer whales number fewer than 100 individuals in the wider Golfo Dulce–Osa area, encountered infrequently but surviving well [@sanchez-robledo-2020-pseudorca], and whale sharks form documented feeding aggregations on the gulf's northwestern shore near river mouths, where terrestrial nutrient input evidently fuels the zooplankton they feed on [@pacheco-polanco-2015-tiburonballena]. Interview-based work suggests whale shark presence is real but strongly pulsed in time, since a dedicated two-and-a-half-month field survey recorded zero sightings against a local-knowledge estimate of up to 226 individuals across 2001–2007 [@hanna-2016-rhincodon].

Two sea turtle species use the gulf as a foraging ground rather than a nesting site. In-water capture work identified Golfo Dulce as an important feeding area for the critically endangered hawksbill, with juveniles dominating the catch and detection notably higher in the dry season, plausibly tied to river-driven turbidity [@chacon-chaverri-2015-carey]. A companion study of green turtles recorded international connectivity — one recaptured female had originally been tagged in the Galápagos — and proposed the gulf's mangrove, reef and seagrass mosaic as the resource base drawing turtles in [@chacon-chaverri-2015b-verde]. A broad two-season baseline survey of marine megafauna generally (458 sightings, at least 30 species, 423.7 observation hours) mapped where whales, dolphins, turtles and sea snakes concentrate, and confirmed that the gulf functions as habitat for foraging, mating, resting and socializing rather than a corridor animals merely pass through [@bessesen-2015-vertebrados]. A separate test of how much locally sourced knowledge can be trusted — repeating fisher and guide interviews a decade apart against independent boat surveys — found local ecological knowledge a genuinely useful, though imperfect, complement to direct observation for these same species [@bessesen-2021-lek].

## Mangroves: the gulf's own stands, and Térraba-Sierpe to the north

Golfo Dulce's own mangrove stands are modest in area but structurally distinct from one another. Transect surveys of three stands — Playa Blanca, Escondido and Rincón de Osa — found Rhizophora racemosa and Pelliciera rhizophorae dominant everywhere, as expected regionally, but the stands differed sharply in canopy height, density and composition: Playa Blanca had the tallest canopy and lowest density, Escondido the densest, shortest canopy and the only stand with Conocarpus erectus present [@samper-villarreal-2015-manglares]. The authors' conclusion is a useful caution: variation within the gulf exceeds the similarity to the regional mangrove pattern, so "the Golfo Dulce mangrove" is not one thing. These mangroves are also a working fishery. A three-year population study of Anadara tuberculosa, the piangua cockle harvested by hand throughout the gulf's mangroves, found densities differing twentyfold between two sites and mean shell length at the more heavily harvested site already below Costa Rica's legal minimum — direct evidence of growth overfishing [@silva-benavides-2015-anadara]. The only dedicated fish-community survey of a Golfo Dulce mangrove, at Zancudo, recorded 82 species across 30 families, more than half estuarine transients using the mangrove as a nursery and transit corridor rather than a permanent home, and found 71% of species captured there carry commercial value — meaning mangrove loss converts almost directly into lost catch [@feutry-2010-zancudo].

Just north of the Osa Peninsula sits the Térraba-Sierpe National Wetland, Costa Rica's largest mangrove system and a Ramsar site, at roughly 14,800 hectares — an order of magnitude larger than anything inside Golfo Dulce itself, built by river delta processes rather than by a sheltered embayment. Satellite classification there mapped the same core species pool (Rhizophora racemosa, Pelliciera rhizophorae, Rhizophora mangle, plus the fern Acrostichum aureum) across that much larger area [@acuna-piedra-2018-terraba]. Together, the two systems make a useful comparison: a large, delta-fed mangrove complex just outside the gulf and a set of small, individually distinct stands inside it.

## Coral and reef presence

Reefs persist inside this sediment-loaded, fjord-like basin, though not as the coral-dominated systems found on more open coastlines. A standardized resurvey of 27 sites across the wider Osa Conservation Area, including Golfo Dulce, found turf algae dominant (62.7% cover) and live coral cover moderate overall (16.5%), but recorded significant coral-cover recovery at Golfo Dulce and nearby Caño Island relative to earlier surveys, and found Golfo Dulce held the highest macroinvertebrate diversity and density of any locality surveyed [@alvarado-2015-acosa]. The gulf's reef-fish community is also compositionally distinct from the rest of the conservation area, not simply a thinner version of the open-coast fauna. A shallow-water fish census at the La Viuda rocky islet recorded 28 species over four quarterly surveys, dominated by damselfishes and wrasses, with diversity tracking season and wave exposure [@palacios-martinez-2015-viuda]. The deepest paleoenvironmental record from the gulf comes from the Punta Islotes fringing reef, where sediment cores show at least 9 m of reef accumulation beginning around 5,500 years ago, with growth rates that collapsed in the most recent phase — attributed first to freshwater input and, more recently, to sediment from deforestation in the surrounding catchments [@cortes-1991-arrecifes]. Reefs here sit close to communities that depend on and value them: structured interviews with 183 fishers, tourism operators and residents found tourism workers the best-informed group about reef ecology, and more than 60% of both fishers and tourism operators independently naming sedimentation and agrochemical contamination as the principal threat — matching what the biophysical surveys report directly [@villalobos-cubero-2023-arrecifes].

Golfo Dulce also holds an ecologically unrelated but geologically connected curiosity: a shallow cold methane seep, discovered at 10 m depth, venting gas through a carbonate platform in otherwise fully oxygenated water — a combination previously known only from deep hydrothermal settings [@wild-2015-methane-seep]. Follow-up characterization of the seep site recorded 18 fish species and 93 cryptofauna taxa forming a community unlike anything else documented on the Costa Rican Pacific coast [@salas-2023-gas-seeps]. It is a reminder that the gulf's chemistry produces unusual biological neighborhoods at more than one depth and by more than one mechanism.

## Fisheries

Golfo Dulce's artisanal fisheries are shaped as much by the gulf's oxygen structure as by fish behavior, because the deeper bottom is largely unfishable. A survey of ray-fishing operations recorded that catches concentrate over muddy bottoms in water shallower than 30 m across the named fishing grounds — Los Bajos, Pique Fijo, La Ciénaga, Lapa-Ríos, Piedra del Sombrero, Pavones — a direct statement of where the demersal fishery can physically operate above a basin floor that is hypoxic to anoxic below [@lopez-garro-2015b-dasyatis]. Sharks and rays together made up more than half of the catch monitored across 67 artisanal bottom-longline trips (2010–2011), with the scalloped hammerhead alone accounting for just over half of all sharks recorded, nearly all of them juveniles [@lopez-garro-2016-tiburones]. A focused study of that hammerhead catch found all 315 individuals sampled were juveniles, averaging 74.3 cm in length, with the smallest animals — and highest catch rates — appearing together in a July-August pulse consistent with a pupping season, leading the authors to propose seasonal closures at the specific sites where catches concentrate [@zanella-2016-martillo]. Fish community work ties the same shallow-water constraint to species richness directly: a synthesis of four decades of trawl surveys found 36 flatfish species between 8 and 359 m overall, but of the 19 species recorded specifically in Golfo Dulce, 58% were taken below 100 m and were judged probably exposed to hypoxic conditions there [@vargas-2020-flatfish] — the same depth boundary that pins the artisanal fishery to the shallows pins flatfish diversity to the water just below it.

## How the oxygen structure shapes everything below the surface

This is the feature that makes Golfo Dulce worth a dedicated section, and it needs framing carefully. The gulf is not permanently anoxic — recent oceanographic work describes the deep basin as frequently, not continuously, anoxic, with the boundary shifting with sill-overflow events and seasonal conditions [@vargas-zamora-2021-fiordo]. What the ecological record shows is not a dead zone but a moving boundary that compresses and excludes life as it deepens.

Zooplankton is the clearest case. Stratified sampling during the 1997–1998 El Niño found the community sharply concentrated in the upper water layer at every station, with mean abundance far higher above the thermocline than below it and a markedly impoverished deep layer throughout [@quesada-alpizar-2006-zooplancton]. Finer-grained day-and-night sampling of two dominant ostracod species found them overwhelmingly confined to the top 20 m, with only 2% and 0.4% of individuals, respectively, found below 50 m — and their earliest developmental stages essentially never left the surface layer at all [@morales-ramirez-2008-ostracodos]. That has a specific consequence for a behavior that is close to universal elsewhere in the ocean: diel vertical migration, where zooplankton move down by day to avoid predators and up by night to feed, is truncated here, because the water column below the near-surface layer is not somewhere the animals can survive migrating into. A 2005–2007 survey measuring oxygen directly at 70–140 m found it was not wholly absent — 0.25 to 1.10 mg/L — but low enough, alongside nitrate enrichment from decomposing sinking organic matter, to explain why the community stays compressed above it [@morales-ramirez-2015-oceanografia].

Demersal fish distribution tracks the same boundary. The flatfish result already mentioned — 58% of species recorded below 100 m in Golfo Dulce and probably exposed to hypoxia there — is one of the few cases in the literature that connects a whole fish group directly to the measured oxygen structure of the water column [@vargas-2020-flatfish].

The most direct test of what actually lives on the basin floor comes from a single 1993 cruise: 26 box cores across nine stations between 43 and 200 m recovered 1,690 individuals in 69 species, overwhelmingly polychaete worms, but the two deepest stations at 200 m yielded no organisms at all, while a diverse fauna was present at comparable depths just outside the sill [@leon-morales-1998-macroinfauna]. It would be easy to read that as proof the basin floor is permanently lifeless, but the same paper complicates that reading on its own terms: compared against a 1976 survey at similar stations, species composition had turned over almost completely while overall diversity stayed similar, and the authors attribute both the turnover and the 1993 azoic result to fluctuating hypoxia-anoxia compounded by El Niño forcing — not to a fixed, permanent absence of life. Read correctly, that 1993 result is a snapshot of one anoxic episode at one moment, not a demonstration that nothing can live there when conditions differ.

One species offers the strongest evidence that the oxygen structure has shaped an entire population's evolution, not just its momentary distribution. A yellow-bodied subspecies of pelagic sea snake, Hydrophis platurus xanthos, is restricted to roughly 320 km² of the gulf's inner basin — water the describing paper itself characterizes as occasionally anoxic — and found nowhere else, despite the parent species ranging across the entire tropical Indo-Pacific and eastern Pacific [@bessesen-2017-xanthos]. The authors propose that the shallow sill separating the inner basin from the open Pacific restricts gene flow with the wider oceanic population, effectively isolating this population behind a bathymetric barrier the way an island isolates land animals. It is described from 154 specimens with distinctive behavior unlike anything reported elsewhere for the species, including nocturnal ambush feeding in turbulent water.

Even the chemistry of decay is coupled to the plankton community here in an unusual way. Isotope-labeling experiments on sinking carcasses of the gulf's abundant copepods and ostracods showed that dead zooplankton carry their own anoxic microenvironments down through the water column, hosting intense anaerobic nitrogen-cycling — denitrification, ammonium production, and anammox — even in water where ambient oxygen is present [@stief-2017-carcasas]. That extends a much larger finding: anaerobic ammonium oxidation, previously known mainly from sediments and engineered wastewater reactors, was first shown to matter in an open marine water column in Golfo Dulce, where it was found to account for 19–35% of nitrogen gas production in the anoxic layer — a result significant enough that it made this small Costa Rican basin a proof of concept cited in essentially every subsequent study of nitrogen loss in the ocean's larger oxygen minimum zones [@dalsgaard-2003-anammox]. The sinking-carcass finding shows that same process is not confined to the deep water column: it travels downward attached to individual dead animals, seeding anoxic chemistry into water that would otherwise be oxygenated.

The public occurrence record for this whole zone remains thin, as this project's own OBIS analysis shows directly: barely a few dozen depth-tagged records exist below 100 m in the entire dataset. That is a gap in observation — covered in more depth on this site's gap register — not a claim that the deep gulf is empty. What the assembled evidence does support is a consistent pattern across independent lines of work — zooplankton, ostracods, flatfish, box-core macrofauna, an endemic sea snake, and the nitrogen chemistry of sinking carcasses — of life compressed toward the surface and excluded with depth, tracking a boundary that itself moves rather than sitting fixed at one line on a chart.
