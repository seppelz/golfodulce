Golfo Dulce's human history is unevenly documented. Some chapters — the banana
company's arrival, the land conflicts that followed — are covered by careful
archival research. Others, especially anything from before Spanish contact and
anything underwater, are thin: not because nothing happened, but because
comparatively little looking has been done.

## Before memory: pre-Columbian Osa and the Diquís spheres

The best-known pre-Columbian remains near Golfo Dulce are the stone spheres of
the Diquís Delta, northwest of the gulf across the Osa peninsula. Four sites —
Finca 6, Batambal, El Silencio and Grijalba-2 — form a UNESCO World Heritage
property, attributed to chiefdom societies occupying the delta roughly AD
500–1500, with spheres 0.7 to 2.57 metres across, most preserved only because
river sediment buried and shielded them from the looting that stripped other
sites bare [@unesco-2014-diquis]. It was United Fruit's own land-clearing in
the delta during the 1930s–40s that exposed most of them, and the spheres have
carried a political life ever since, as national symbol and as a rallying
point for the plantation community that found them [@desimone-2014-esferas].

The gulf's own shoreline archaeology is thinner. The Museo Nacional's account
of Osa settlement states the peninsula has been very lightly surveyed despite
gold-bearing rivers pre-Columbian societies exploited; it identifies two
occupation periods, Aguas Buenas (300 BC–AD 800) and Chiriquí (AD 800–1500),
names the mound site of Cantarero as the best-studied settlement, and admits no
systematic regional survey has ever been completed — it took looting damage at
Cantarero in 2015 to trigger the museum's current work [@corrales-2025-osa].
The only monograph on the gulf shoreline itself, rather than the delta or the
Osa interior, is a 1999 UCR study of Playa Cacao and the Alto Purruja site,
across the bay from Golfito; it was never digitised and requires an
in-person library request [@arias-1999-golfodulce]. Even the population at
contact is disputed: the often-repeated figure of ~400,000 indigenous people in
Costa Rica has been challenged by a reconstruction arguing for closer to
27,000, with the Osa/Golfo Dulce coast among the thinnest-occupied stretches
either way [@solorzano-2017-poblacion].

Gold is the clearer thread: placer deposits were worked "since pre-Colombian
times," and by the 1980s the Golfo Dulce goldfield produced at least twice as
much gold as Costa Rica's entire northern gold province [@berrange-1992-placergold].
Where that gold ended up — the ore behind the elaborate Chiriquí-period
goldwork in Costa Rican museums — is only now being traced through
mining-archaeology and geochemical provenance work, explicit that very little
was previously known about pre-conquest mining here [@gassmann-2024-oro].
Separate microscopy on carved greenstone pendants elsewhere in the south found
technique variation consistent with multiple artisan groups rather than one
workshop — the same open question hanging over who made and moved the Diquís
spheres [@kuboyama-2023-lapidaria].

## Indigenous peoples and the water

Today's Boruca/Brunca, Térraba (Brörán) and Ngäbe (Guaymí) communities live
under the 1977 Ley Indígena, itself the outcome of a debate over land rights
reaching back to the late 1930s [@boza-2025-leyindigena]. Boruca and
Curré/Yimba now run their own community museums as acts of self-determination
rather than heritage display [@martinez-2024-museos], and the Térraba have
documented how their territory on the Río Grande de Térraba — the river system
whose delta sediment passes the Diquís sites toward the coast beside Golfo
Dulce — was reshaped by labour and land struggle between 1940 and 2015
[@chensui-2018-terraba]. The fullest quantitative record of Ngäbe environmental
knowledge comes from the Coto Brus reserve, inland of the Golfo Dulce
watershed, where fieldwork recorded 53 wild food plant species valued above
all from mature forest [@castaneda-2007-guaymi].

What is missing is any comparable record of these peoples' relationship
specifically to the gulf's water — its fisheries, its mangroves, its use as a
travel corridor. The sources located describe forest knowledge inland of the
watershed and land struggle on the river, not the coast itself. The closest
methodological bridge is work testing how well gulf fishers' and guides' local
knowledge holds up against independent boat surveys of whales, turtles and sea
snakes a decade apart — proof that such knowledge can be documented and
calibrated, even though it has not yet been gathered from the gulf's
indigenous communities [@bessesen-2021-lek].

## Spanish charts, and a gulf with no recorded shipwreck

Golfo Dulce entered European hydrography as a boundary marker, not a subject.
The Spanish Hydrographic Office's 1822 chart running north from the gulf to San
Blas, built on the 1789–1794 Malaspina expedition, fixes the coastline at a
scale where the whole embayment spans a few millimetres, without sounding its
floor [@direcciondehidrografia-1822-cartaesferica]. Two centuries on, coverage
is not much better: the only dedicated chart of the gulf, US chart 21562, was
last corrected in 1998 and has since been cancelled and withdrawn
[@nga-1998-chart21562], while the British Admiralty gives the gulf a single
1:100,000 inset — a quarter the scale devoted to the pier at Golfito itself
[@ukho-2026-chart2493]. Costa Rica's own 2024 report to the regional
hydrographic commission states it has no dedicated hydrographic office and no
vessels for large-scale survey [@costarica-2024-informe]; the international
charting register records that 90% of Costa Rican waters under 200 m, and 95%
of deeper waters, still require re-survey, with port approaches, not remote
fjord basins, the stated priority [@iho-2026-c55].

Against that backdrop, one fact deserves stating precisely. The Museo
Nacional's inventory of investigated historic shipwrecks lists eight vessels
nationally, in the Golfo de Nicoya and on the Caribbean coast; none is in
Golfo Dulce or anywhere on the southern Pacific [@museocostarica-2024-naufragios].
That absence is real, but it is evidence of one specific thing — no survey has
been done — not evidence that nothing is there. The same source notes Costa
Rica only ratified the UNESCO underwater cultural heritage convention in 2018,
making systematic underwater archaeology genuinely young here, and that its
own wreck register is explicitly incomplete [@museocostarica-2024-naufragios].
The only underwater archaeological fieldwork so far carried out on Costa
Rica's Pacific coast is at Isla del Coco, roughly 550 km offshore, where a
wreck tentatively identified as the SV Viduco has been located and studied
[@fernandez-2020-viduco]. A gulf whose deep basin runs low-oxygen or anoxic
for long stretches is, if anything, a promising place to look: the conditions
that exclude bottom life are exactly the conditions under which wooden hull
material and rigging, which rot quickly elsewhere, could plausibly survive
[@museocostarica-2024-naufragios]. The gulf's archaeological potential was in
fact already flagged once, in that 1999 UCR study around Playa Cacao and
Golfito that was never followed up in print [@arias-1999-golfodulce].

## The banana era: United Fruit and the making of Golfito

Before the company arrived, Osa was sparsely settled: sixteenth- and
seventeenth-century Spanish colonies here failed, and it was the 1840s before
chiricano migrants founded Puerto Jiménez, sustaining a marginal trade in
dried beef, lard, coconut oil and pearl shell into the 1930s while the state
ran failed colonisation schemes aimed at holding the territory against
Colombian claims [@hombergh-1999-guerreros]. That changed in 1938, when the
1934 banana contract brought United Fruit to the Pacific coast at Golfito and
Palmar [@harpelle-2000-enclave; hombergh-1999-guerreros]. The contract barred
"people of colour" from Pacific employment, part of a wider campaign against
the Afro-Caribbean community of Limón — closing English schools, evicting
farmers, deporting West Indians — which is why Golfito's workforce looked
nothing like Limón's from the outset [@harpelle-2000-enclave]. Golfito grew as
two towns: a fenced company zone, and the unplanned "Pueblo Civil" outside it
that the state, not the company, had to build and service, with the housing
institute INVU taking over from 1954 [@conejo-2025-golfito]. Elderly residents
interviewed decades later describe local identity as resting jointly on the
bay's natural setting and on the twentieth-century banana infrastructure
itself, which they treat as heritage rather than industrial leftover
[@riba-2018-golfito].

## After bananas: oil palm, land reform, and who owns the shore

United Fruit withdrew from the Pacific in 1984–85, and African oil palm
replaced bananas as an ecological regime change — different soils, disease
pressure and labour needs — rather than a simple crop swap [@clare-2005-palma].
Land did not simply revert to former occupants: in Palmar Sur, the old United
Fruit district in the delta, the land-reform agency's shift from the
Instituto de Desarrollo Agrario to the Instituto de Desarrollo Rural has been
read as manufacturing "entrepreneurial" peasant subjects while consolidating a
new dispossession, regulating land access through conduct rather than title
[@guillen-2020-palmarsur]. It is worth resisting too tidy a story: a
canton-level study of Osa argues the banana enclave, dated 1941–1984, was only
one of several causes of regional poverty, alongside agrarian structural
limits and factors external to the territory [@royo-2009-osa]. The gulf's
2010 fishery management plan profiles who works the water today — seven
petitioning fisher and sport-fishing associations, with membership, finances
and household socio-economic conditions in the fishing communities
[@incopesca-2010-ordenamiento] — and a Stanford-led regional synthesis frames
those communities' traditional and newer uses of the gulf as already under
real pressure despite low population density [@molina-bustamante-2014-inogo].
More recent development disputes — a marina at Puerto Jiménez, a chip-export
port and gmelina plantation on Osa — have produced an organised response: a
coalition of the Frente Nacional por la Protección de los Humedales and the
Frente por el Golfo Dulce, combining community organisations and technical
advocacy to contest what gets built on the shore [@aguilar-2013-defensa].
