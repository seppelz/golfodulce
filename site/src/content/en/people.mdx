Golfo Dulce's human history is unevenly documented. The banana company's
arrival and the land conflicts that followed are covered by careful archival
research; everything from before Spanish contact, and anything underwater, is
thin — not because nothing happened, but because little looking has been
done.

## Before memory: pre-Columbian Osa and the Diquís spheres

The best-known pre-Columbian remains near Golfo Dulce are the stone spheres of
the Diquís Delta, northwest of the gulf across the Osa peninsula. Four sites —
Finca 6, Batambal, El Silencio and Grijalba-2 — form a UNESCO World Heritage
property, attributed to chiefdom societies occupying the delta roughly AD
500–1500, with spheres 0.7 to 2.57 metres across, most preserved only because
river sediment buried and shielded them from the looting that stripped other
sites bare [@unesco-2014-diquis]. It was United Fruit's own land-clearing in
the delta during the 1930s–40s that exposed most of them, and the spheres
have carried a political life ever since, as national symbol and as a
rallying point for the plantation community that found them
[@desimone-2014-esferas].

The gulf's own shoreline archaeology is thinner. The Museo Nacional's account
of Osa settlement states the peninsula has been very lightly surveyed despite
gold-bearing rivers pre-Columbian societies exploited; it identifies two
occupation periods, Aguas Buenas (300 BC–AD 800) and Chiriquí (AD 800–1500),
names the mound site of Cantarero as the best-studied settlement, and admits
no systematic regional survey has ever been completed — it took looting
damage at Cantarero in 2015 to trigger the museum's current work
[@corrales-2025-osa]. The only monograph on the gulf shoreline itself, rather
than the delta or the Osa interior, is a 1999 UCR study of Playa Cacao and
the Alto Purruja site, across the bay from Golfito, never digitised and
reachable only by in-person library request [@arias-1999-golfodulce]. Even
contact-era population is disputed, the often-repeated ~400,000 indigenous
people in Costa Rica challenged by a reconstruction arguing for closer to
27,000 [@solorzano-2017-poblacion]. Gold is a clearer thread: placer deposits
here were worked "since pre-Colombian times," and where it ended up — the
Chiriquí-period goldwork now in Costa Rican museums — is only now being
traced, through work explicit about how little was previously known of
pre-conquest mining here [@berrange-1992-placergold; gassmann-2024-oro].

## Indigenous peoples and the water

Today's Boruca/Brunca, Térraba (Brörán) and Ngäbe (Guaymí) communities live
under the 1977 Ley Indígena, itself the outcome of a debate over land rights
reaching back to the late 1930s [@boza-2025-leyindigena]. Boruca and
Curré/Yimba run their own community museums as acts of self-determination
rather than heritage display [@martinez-2024-museos], and the Térraba have
documented how their territory on the Río Grande de Térraba — whose delta
sediment passes the Diquís sites toward the coast beside Golfo Dulce — was
reshaped by labour and land struggle between 1940 and 2015
[@chensui-2018-terraba]. The fullest quantitative record of Ngäbe
environmental knowledge comes from the Coto Brus reserve, inland of the
watershed, where fieldwork recorded 53 wild food plant species valued above
all from mature forest [@castaneda-2007-guaymi].

What is missing is any comparable record of these peoples' relationship to
the gulf's water itself — fisheries, mangroves, travel by boat. The sources
located describe forest knowledge inland of the watershed and land struggle
on the river, not the coast. The closest bridge is work testing gulf
fishers' and guides' local knowledge against independent boat surveys a
decade apart, proof such knowledge can be documented and calibrated — though
not yet gathered from indigenous communities specifically [@bessesen-2021-lek].

## Spanish charts, and a gulf with no recorded shipwreck

Golfo Dulce entered European hydrography as a boundary marker, not a
subject: the Spanish Hydrographic Office's 1822 chart running north from the
gulf to San Blas, built on the 1789–1794 Malaspina expedition, fixes the
coastline at a scale where the embayment spans a few millimetres, without
sounding its floor [@direcciondehidrografia-1822-cartaesferica]. Two
centuries on, coverage is barely better: the only dedicated chart of the
gulf, US chart 21562, was last corrected in 1998 and has since been cancelled
[@nga-1998-chart21562], and the British Admiralty gives the gulf one
1:100,000 inset — a quarter the scale devoted to the pier at Golfito
[@ukho-2026-chart2493]. Costa Rica has no dedicated hydrographic office and
no vessels for large-scale survey [@costarica-2024-informe]; internationally,
90% of its waters under 200 m, and 95% of deeper waters, are recorded as
still requiring re-survey, with port approaches, not remote fjord basins, the
stated priority [@iho-2026-c55].

One fact deserves stating precisely. The Museo Nacional's inventory of
investigated historic shipwrecks lists eight vessels nationally, in the Golfo
de Nicoya and on the Caribbean coast; none is in Golfo Dulce or the southern
Pacific [@museocostarica-2024-naufragios]. That absence is
evidence of one specific thing — no survey has been done — not that nothing
is there: the same source notes Costa Rica only ratified the UNESCO
underwater cultural heritage convention in 2018, making underwater
archaeology genuinely young here, and that its own wreck register is
explicitly incomplete [@museocostarica-2024-naufragios]. The only underwater
archaeological fieldwork so far on the Pacific coast is at Isla del Coco,
roughly 550 km offshore, where a wreck tentatively identified as the SV
Viduco has been studied [@fernandez-2020-viduco]. A gulf whose deep basin runs low-oxygen or anoxic
for long stretches is, if anything, promising to look at: those are exactly
the conditions under which wooden hull material and rigging, which rot
quickly elsewhere, could plausibly survive [@museocostarica-2024-naufragios].
The potential was flagged once already, in the 1999 UCR study around Playa
Cacao and Golfito never followed up in print [@arias-1999-golfodulce].

## The banana era: United Fruit and the making of Golfito

Before the company arrived, Osa was sparsely settled: Spanish colonies here
failed in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and only in the 1840s did
chiricano migrants found Puerto Jiménez, sustaining a marginal trade in dried
beef, lard, coconut oil and pearl shell into the 1930s [@hombergh-1999-guerreros].
That changed in 1938, when the 1934 banana contract brought United Fruit to
Golfito and Palmar [@harpelle-2000-enclave; hombergh-1999-guerreros]. The
contract barred
"people of colour" from Pacific employment, part of a wider campaign against
the Afro-Caribbean community of Limón — closing English schools, evicting
farmers, deporting West Indians — which is why Golfito's workforce looked
nothing like Limón's from the outset [@harpelle-2000-enclave].
Golfito grew as two towns: a fenced company zone, and the unplanned "Pueblo
Civil" the state, not the company, had to build and service, with the
housing institute INVU taking over from 1954 [@conejo-2025-golfito]. Elderly
residents interviewed decades later treat that banana infrastructure itself
as local heritage, not industrial leftover [@riba-2018-golfito].

## After bananas: oil palm, land reform, and who owns the shore

United Fruit withdrew from the Pacific in 1984–85, and African oil palm
replaced bananas as an ecological regime change — different soils, disease
pressure, labour needs — rather than a simple crop swap [@clare-2005-palma].
Land did not simply revert to former occupants: in Palmar Sur, the old
United Fruit district in the delta, the land-reform agency's shift from the
Instituto de Desarrollo Agrario to the Instituto de Desarrollo Rural has been
read as manufacturing "entrepreneurial" peasant subjects while consolidating
a new dispossession, regulating land access through conduct rather than
title [@guillen-2020-palmarsur]. It is worth resisting too tidy a story
here: a canton-level study of Osa argues the banana enclave, dated
1941–1984, was only one of several causes of regional poverty, alongside
agrarian structural limits and factors external to the territory
[@royo-2009-osa].

Who works the gulf's water today is documented in its 2010 fishery
management plan, profiling seven petitioning fisher associations by
membership, finances and household conditions [@incopesca-2010-ordenamiento],
and in a Stanford-led synthesis framing those communities' uses of the gulf
as already under real pressure despite low population density
[@molina-bustamante-2014-inogo]. Recent development disputes — a marina at
Puerto Jiménez, a chip-export port and plantation on Osa — have produced an
organised response: a coalition of local groups contesting what gets built on
the shore [@aguilar-2013-defensa].
