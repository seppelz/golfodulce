## A fjord with no ice in its history

Golfo Dulce has the shape of a fjord: a deep, steep-walled inner basin over 200 m deep, separated from the open ocean by a shallow sill around 60 m deep — the same basic geometry as Norway's or Chile's glacially carved coastlines [@hebbeln-1996-morphology] [@quesada-2006-ecosistemas]. The puzzle is that it sits at roughly 8.5° north, well within the tropics, and was never glaciated. Classic fjords are glacial troughs, valleys gouged by ice sheets that later flooded as sea level rose. No ice sheet has ever reached Costa Rica. Whatever carved this basin, it was not ice, which leaves tectonics as the only candidate — and it is here that a genuine, unresolved scientific disagreement begins: geologists agree the basin is fault-controlled, but not on which fault mechanism actually built it.

## Tectonic setting: a peninsula riding a subducting ridge

Golfo Dulce sits on the southern Pacific margin of Costa Rica, where the Cocos oceanic plate subducts beneath the Caribbean plate at the Middle America Trench. The Cocos plate arriving here carries the Cocos Ridge, an unusually thick, aseismic swell of crust from the Galápagos hotspot, whose subduction has flattened the descending slab and driven rapid, geologically very recent uplift of the peninsulas flanking the gulf [@kolarsky-1995-islandarc] [@gardner-2013-flatslab]. Radiocarbon-dated Holocene marine terraces on Osa record uplift decreasing from about 6.5 mm/yr near the ridge's point of maximum impact to 2.1 mm/yr further away, with the peninsula behaving as fault blocks tilting down toward the northeast — toward the gulf, part of why Golfo Dulce is deepest on its inner side rather than near its mouth [@gardner-1992-uplift]. Burica, the gulf's southeastern wall, shows comparable uplift, with terraces as young as 60,000 years now standing above sea level [@morell-2011-burica]. Osa is not one rising block but is fragmented into independently moving pieces only a few kilometres across, each responding to the seamounts on the plate directly beneath it [@gardner-2013-flatslab].

The basement itself has a layered history. What was once lumped in with the "Nicoya Complex" is now recognised as a distinct terrane, the Osa Igneous Complex — accreted fragments of oceanic plateau and seamounts, exotic to the Caribbean plate, added on episodically between the Late Cretaceous and Miocene [@buchs-2009-seamounts] [@berrange-1992-placergold]. Above it sits the Osa Mélange, formed when the trench switched from accreting material onto the margin to eroding it away, coinciding with the thickened Cocos Ridge crust arriving at the Miocene–Pliocene boundary — the same switch that fractured the outer forearc into the block-faulted mosaic still visible around the gulf today [@vannucchi-2006-melange]. The system stays seismically active: earthquakes nucleate where subducting seamounts concentrate stress on the plate interface [@martinez-loriente-2019-relief] [@moyer-2011-stress], and archival work has recovered a large, historically overlooked earthquake in 1879 centred on the gulf shoreline itself [@peraldo-2006-terremoto].

## The origin debate: pull-apart basin, or doming and thrusting?

Two entirely different explanations for the basin's own origin have been argued in print, and they have not been reconciled.

Jevan Berrangé, writing in the 1980s, places Golfo Dulce within a system of NW-trending dextral wrench faults — a wrench fault being a strike-slip fault where the two sides slide horizontally past each other — that he reads as the onshore continuation of the Panama–Coiba Fracture Zone. In his model the gulf is a modern pull-apart basin: a rhomb-shaped depression that opens where a strike-slip fault system steps sideways, stretching and dropping the ground between the steps. He draws an explicit comparison to the Gulf of California, a textbook pull-apart system, with Osa and Burica as tilted "mini-horsts" flanking the down-dropped gulf [@berrange-1987-gasseeps]. A companion paper extends this into the rock record: the Pliocene Osa Group, a thick clastic sequence exposed on the peninsula, is read as sediment that accumulated inside subsiding pull-apart basins under this same wrench-fault regime, with Golfo Dulce as its still-active modern equivalent [@berrange-1989-osagroup].

Stanislav Kriz, writing in 1990, disputes this directly. In his reading, the whole region — Osa, Burica and the ground between — was a single, continuous island from the Early Miocene until the Late Pleistocene, raised as a broad anticlinal dome in old oceanic crust. Only much more recently did that dome break apart, and Kriz attributes the break-up to thrusting — older basement pushed up and over its own younger cover along structures he names the Osa Reverse Fault and the Eastern Golfo Dulce Fault — not to strike-slip extension. He states outright that "no 'pull-apart' Pliocene basins exist in the Golfo Dulce area, nor do certain right-lateral strike-slip faults related to the Panama Fracture Zone" [@kriz-1990-placers] — a direct rejection of Berrangé's mechanism, not a variation on it.

Neither model has been cleanly superseded; later regional work has largely moved the conversation onto different ground rather than settling this specific dispute. Corrigan, Mann and Ingle documented rapid Pliocene subsidence on Burica — a roughly 3,000 m thick turbidite sequence accumulating in deep water — followed by shoaling and Quaternary uplift, and named the Ballena–Celmira fault zone as the structure absorbing vertical displacement along this margin [@corrigan-1990-forearc]. Gardner and colleagues quantified Osa's Quaternary uplift and northeastward tilt as flexural responses to the subducting ridge [@gardner-1992-uplift] [@gardner-2013-flatslab], and Vannucchi et al. tied the outer forearc's fragmentation into independently moving blocks to the trench's switch from accretion to tectonic erosion [@vannucchi-2006-melange]. None of these papers directly tests the pull-apart-versus-doming question; they describe a margin dominated by ridge-subduction-driven uplift and block faulting, which sits closer in spirit to Kriz's thrust-and-block picture than to Berrangé's strike-slip one, without confirming either in detail. A reasonable synthesis — offered here, not a citable published consensus — is that Golfo Dulce is a fault-bounded depression inside a rapidly rising, block-faulted forearc, shaped by some mix of oblique convergence, differential uplift and reactivated older structures, rather than by a clean strike-slip pull-apart or a simple dome-and-thrust in isolation. Whether the depression's dominant kinematics are strike-slip, as Berrangé argued, or reverse, as Kriz argued, remains unresolved in the published literature.

## Sediment: what the one core study actually found

Only one study has directly cored and analysed what is accumulating on the gulf floor. Hebbeln and Cortés collected surface samples and 3.5 m gravity and piston cores across the basin and found the fill overwhelmingly terrigenous — more than 90% land-derived rather than produced by marine organisms, with even the organic carbon and carbonate traceable to different stretches of the surrounding coast [@hebbeln-2001-sedimentation]. The critical negative result: the cores held no laminated sections at all. Instead the sediment splits into turbiditic deposits on the flat basin floor, delivered by episodic underwater gravity flows down channels from the slopes, and largely undisturbed hemipelagic deposits on the slopes themselves [@hebbeln-2001-sedimentation]. That matters because it means Golfo Dulce does not behave like the Black Sea or Venezuela's Cariaco Basin, both prized as climate archives because their stratified anoxic waters exclude burrowing animals and preserve fine, undisturbed annual layers (varves) for millennia. Golfo Dulce is periodically anoxic, but turbidity currents are actively reworking its inner basin, so any signal that might otherwise be locked into an undisturbed layered record is instead being physically mixed and redeposited. The preservation potential should not be oversold on the strength of the anoxia alone — the sedimentology is working against it, at least in the shallow, likely late-Holocene section sampled so far [@hebbeln-2001-sedimentation].

That single coring study sits inside a broader pattern of thin coverage. The only acoustic survey of the basin floor, fourteen single-beam echosounder lines run from the R/V Victor Hensen in 1993–94, produced published figures rather than an archived dataset: nothing from that cruise appears to have been deposited at NCEI, PANGAEA or GMRT [@hebbeln-1996-echosounder]. PANGAEA, the standard open archive for marine sediment and geophysical data, holds no sediment-core datasets for this gulf at all — the record that exists is locked inside a single 2001 paper's figures and tables, not in a reusable public archive, so the basin's sedimentary history remains effectively unexploited [@hebbeln-2001-sedimentation] [@hebbeln-1996-echosounder]. Onshore, the sediment story is more legible: ten small fan deltas built by steep streams along Bahía de Golfito are read as an active geomorphic signature of ongoing Osa uplift combined with progressive infilling of the bay [@alvarado-1991-fandeltas], and shallow methane seepage, traced most recently to sandstone of the Charco Azul Group, shows gas as well as sediment actively moving through the system today [@salas-2023-gas-seeps] [@wild-2015-methane-seep].

## Sea level and the paleo-coastline

The one place a longer, dated record has actually been recovered from inside the gulf is not the anoxic basin floor but its fringing reef. Cortés cored the Punta Islotes reef down to over 9 m of accumulated coral growing on an uneven Cretaceous basalt surface, with radiocarbon dating placing the start of reef growth at roughly 5,500 years before present — after postglacial sea-level rise had brought the ocean close to its modern position [@cortes-1991-arrecifes]. Accumulation rates varied sharply by reef facies, and the record shows four growth stages, the most recent showing a marked slowdown attributed first to freshwater input and, more recently, to terrigenous sediment from deforestation in the catchment [@cortes-1991-arrecifes]. This is the closest thing Golfo Dulce has to a direct, dated record of how the modern coastline came to be where it is.

The wider tectonic record supplies what sea-level history alone cannot: this coastline has not simply drowned passively as the ocean rose after the last glaciation — it has also been actively lifted and eroded from below. Three-dimensional seismic mapping of the shelf and slope offshore Osa resolves several distinct Pleistocene episodes, around 2.5–2.3, 1.95–1.78 and 1.78–1.19 million years ago, when parts of the shelf were subaerially exposed and eroded before subsiding again, driven by the migrating Panama Fracture Zone triple junction and by subducting seamount topography [@edwards-2018-vertical]. Those episodes lie well outside human timescales and offshore of the gulf proper, but they set the governing principle for reading any local paleo-shoreline: because uplift on the surrounding peninsulas is fast enough to raise marine terraces by metres within a single glacial cycle [@morell-2011-burica] [@gardner-2013-flatslab], a former, lower-sea-level coastline around Golfo Dulce cannot be reconstructed from global sea-level curves alone. The land itself has been moving underneath it, unevenly, block by block.
