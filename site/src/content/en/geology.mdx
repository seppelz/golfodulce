## A fjord with no ice in its history

Golfo Dulce has the shape of a fjord: a deep, steep-walled inner basin over 200 m deep, separated from the open ocean by a shallow sill around 60 m deep — the same basic geometry as Norway's or Chile's glacially carved coastlines [@hebbeln-1996-morphology] [@quesada-2006-ecosistemas]. The puzzle is that it sits at roughly 8.5° north, well within the tropics, and was never glaciated. Classic fjords are glacial troughs, gouged by ice sheets that later flooded as sea level rose; no ice sheet has ever reached Costa Rica. Whatever carved this basin, it was not ice, which leaves tectonics as the only candidate — and here a genuine, unresolved disagreement begins: geologists agree the basin is fault-controlled, but not on which fault mechanism actually built it.

## Tectonic setting: a peninsula riding a subducting ridge

Golfo Dulce sits where the Cocos oceanic plate subducts beneath the Caribbean plate at the Middle America Trench. The Cocos plate arriving here carries the Cocos Ridge, an unusually thick, aseismic swell of crust from the Galápagos hotspot, whose subduction has flattened the descending slab and driven rapid, geologically very recent uplift of the peninsulas flanking the gulf [@kolarsky-1995-islandarc] [@gardner-2013-flatslab]. Holocene marine terraces on Osa record uplift decreasing from about 6.5 to 2.1 mm/yr with distance from the ridge, with the peninsula tilting down toward the northeast — toward the gulf, part of why Golfo Dulce is deepest on its inner side rather than near its mouth [@gardner-1992-uplift]. Burica, the gulf's southeastern wall, shows comparable uplift, with terraces as young as 60,000 years now standing above sea level [@morell-2011-burica], and Osa itself is fragmented into pieces only a few kilometres across, each responding to the seamounts on the plate beneath it [@gardner-2013-flatslab]. The basement — once lumped in with the "Nicoya Complex," now recognised as the distinct Osa Igneous Complex, an accreted mass of oceanic plateau and seamount fragments exotic to the Caribbean plate [@buchs-2009-seamounts] [@berrange-1992-placergold] — is overlain by the Osa Mélange, formed as the trench switched from accreting material onto the margin to eroding it away when the thickened ridge crust arrived, the same switch that fractured the forearc into the block-faulted mosaic visible around the gulf today [@vannucchi-2006-melange].

## The origin debate: pull-apart basin, or doming and thrusting?

Two entirely different explanations for the basin's own origin have been argued in print, and they have not been reconciled. Jevan Berrangé, writing in the 1980s, places Golfo Dulce within a system of NW-trending dextral wrench faults — a wrench fault being a strike-slip fault where the two sides slide horizontally past each other — read as the onshore continuation of the Panama–Coiba Fracture Zone. In his model the gulf is a modern pull-apart basin: a rhomb-shaped depression that opens where a strike-slip fault system steps sideways, stretching and dropping the ground between the steps. He draws an explicit comparison to the Gulf of California, a textbook pull-apart system, with Osa and Burica as tilted "mini-horsts" flanking the down-dropped gulf [@berrange-1987-gasseeps]. A companion paper extends this into the rock record: the Pliocene Osa Group, a thick clastic sequence exposed on the peninsula, is read as sediment accumulated inside subsiding pull-apart basins under the same wrench-fault regime, with Golfo Dulce as its still-active modern equivalent [@berrange-1989-osagroup].

Stanislav Kriz, writing in 1990, disputes this directly. In his reading, the whole region — Osa, Burica and the ground between — was a single, continuous island from the Early Miocene until the Late Pleistocene, raised as a broad anticlinal dome in old oceanic crust, which broke apart only much more recently, by thrusting — older basement pushed up and over its own younger cover along structures he names the Osa Reverse Fault and the Eastern Golfo Dulce Fault — not by strike-slip extension. He states outright that "no 'pull-apart' Pliocene basins exist in the Golfo Dulce area, nor do certain right-lateral strike-slip faults related to the Panama Fracture Zone" [@kriz-1990-placers], a direct rejection of Berrangé's mechanism, not a variation on it.

Neither model has been cleanly superseded; later regional work has moved the conversation onto different ground rather than settling the dispute. Corrigan, Mann and Ingle documented Pliocene subsidence followed by Quaternary uplift on Burica [@corrigan-1990-forearc]; Gardner and colleagues attribute Osa's uplift and northeastward tilt to flexure above the subducting ridge [@gardner-1992-uplift] [@gardner-2013-flatslab]; Vannucchi and colleagues tie the forearc's fragmentation into independently moving fault blocks to the switch from accretion to tectonic erosion [@vannucchi-2006-melange]. None of these papers directly tests the pull-apart-versus-doming question; they simply describe a margin dominated by ridge-subduction-driven uplift and block faulting, closer in spirit to Kriz's picture than Berrangé's, without confirming either in detail. A reasonable synthesis — offered here, not a citable consensus — is that Golfo Dulce is a fault-bounded depression inside a rapidly rising, block-faulted forearc, shaped by some mix of convergence, differential uplift and reactivated older structures, rather than a clean pull-apart or a simple dome-and-thrust alone. Whether its dominant kinematics are strike-slip, as Berrangé argued, or reverse, as Kriz argued, remains unresolved.

## Sediment: what the one core study actually found

Only one study has directly cored and analysed what is accumulating on the gulf floor. Hebbeln and Cortés collected surface samples and 3.5 m gravity and piston cores across the basin and found the fill overwhelmingly terrigenous — more than 90% land-derived rather than produced by marine organisms, with even the organic carbon and carbonate traceable to different stretches of the surrounding coast [@hebbeln-2001-sedimentation]. The critical negative result: the cores held no laminated sections at all. The sediment instead splits into turbiditic deposits on the flat basin floor, delivered by episodic underwater gravity flows, and largely undisturbed hemipelagic deposits on the slopes [@hebbeln-2001-sedimentation]. That matters because Golfo Dulce does not behave like the Black Sea or Venezuela's Cariaco Basin, both prized as climate archives because their stratified anoxic waters exclude burrowing animals and preserve fine, undisturbed annual layers (varves) for millennia. Golfo Dulce is periodically anoxic, but turbidity currents are actively reworking its inner basin, so any signal that might otherwise lock into an undisturbed layered record is instead physically mixed and redeposited — the preservation potential should not be oversold on the anoxia alone, at least in the shallow, likely late-Holocene section sampled so far [@hebbeln-2001-sedimentation].

That single coring study sits inside a broader pattern of thin coverage. The only acoustic survey of the basin floor — fourteen single-beam echosounder lines from the R/V Victor Hensen, 1993–94 — produced published figures, not an archived dataset, and nothing from that cruise was deposited at NCEI, PANGAEA or GMRT [@hebbeln-1996-echosounder]. PANGAEA, the standard open archive for marine sediment and geophysical data, holds no sediment-core datasets for this gulf at all: the existing core record is locked inside one 2001 paper's figures and tables, not a reusable archive, so the basin's sedimentary history remains effectively unexploited [@hebbeln-2001-sedimentation] [@hebbeln-1996-echosounder]. Onshore, fan deltas along Bahía de Golfito read as a geomorphic signature of ongoing Osa uplift and infilling of the bay [@alvarado-1991-fandeltas].

## Sea level and the paleo-coastline

The one place a longer, dated record has actually been recovered from inside the gulf is not the anoxic basin floor but its fringing reef. Cortés cored the Punta Islotes reef down to over 9 m of accumulated coral growing on an uneven Cretaceous basalt surface, with radiocarbon dating placing the start of reef growth at roughly 5,500 years before present — after postglacial sea-level rise had brought the ocean close to its modern position [@cortes-1991-arrecifes]. Accumulation rates varied sharply by reef facies across four growth stages, the most recent a marked slowdown attributed first to freshwater input and, more recently, to terrigenous sediment from deforestation in the catchment [@cortes-1991-arrecifes]. This is the closest thing Golfo Dulce has to a direct, dated record of how the modern coastline came to be where it is.

The wider tectonic record supplies what sea-level history alone cannot: this coastline has not simply drowned passively as the ocean rose after the last glaciation — it has also been actively lifted and eroded from below. Seismic mapping of the shelf and slope offshore Osa resolves several distinct Pleistocene episodes when parts of the shelf were subaerially exposed and eroded before subsiding again, driven by the migrating Panama Fracture Zone triple junction and by subducting seamount topography [@edwards-2018-vertical]. Those episodes lie offshore of the gulf proper, but they establish the governing principle for any local paleo-shoreline: because uplift on the surrounding peninsulas is fast enough to raise marine terraces by metres within a single glacial cycle [@morell-2011-burica] [@gardner-2013-flatslab], a former, lower-sea-level coastline here cannot be reconstructed from global sea-level curves alone. The land itself has been moving underneath it, unevenly, block by block.
