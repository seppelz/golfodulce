The Golfo Dulce sits on Costa Rica's southern Pacific coast, tucked behind the Osa
Peninsula. On a map it looks unremarkable — a narrow inlet, easily missed against the
scale of the Pacific. What makes it unusual is not its shape but its plumbing: a deep
inner basin, over 200 metres at its deepest, separated from the open ocean by a shallow
sill that most estimates put well under 100 metres [@vargas-zamora-2021-fiordo]
[@svendsen-2006-sill]. Water that sinks into the inner basin does not exchange freely
with the Pacific. It is topped up only episodically, when conditions let denser ocean
water spill back over the sill [@svendsen-2006-physical-oceanography].

That structure — a fjord-like basin, in the tropics, with no glacier ever involved in
carving it — is genuinely rare. Fjords are usually a story about ice: a glacier gouges a
deep trough, retreats, and the sea fills in behind it. Nothing here was ever glaciated.
The basin's shape instead comes from active tectonics — the Cocos Ridge driving uplift
on the Osa Peninsula, and a history of faulting whose exact mechanism is still argued
over in the geological literature [@berrange-1987-gasseeps]. The resemblance to a fjord
is a coincidence of geometry, not of process.

The consequence of restricted exchange is an oxygen structure unlike most nearshore
water anywhere. Below roughly 100–200 metres, the water column periodically loses its
oxygen almost entirely. It is not a permanent, static dead zone — it comes and goes with
the renewal events at the sill — but when the deep water does go anoxic, it does so
thoroughly enough that it has made this one basin internationally significant for
understanding how nitrogen cycles in oxygen-free water. A study of the anoxic layer here
showed that a single microbial process, anaerobic ammonium oxidation, accounted for
essentially all of the fixed nitrogen lost from the water column — a finding that placed
the Golfo Dulce alongside the Black Sea as one of the reference sites for that kind of
chemistry [@dalsgaard-2003-anammox]. The mechanism that keeps the basin from staying
permanently sulfidic is itself well characterised: frequent, if irregular, inflows of
oxygenated Pacific water hold the worst of it in check [@ferdelman-2006-oxygen-nitrogen-sulfur].

None of that comes with a correspondingly detailed picture of the basin itself. The
global bathymetric compilations most people would reach for — GEBCO, GMRT — cover this
area, but largely by interpolation rather than direct measurement
[@gebco-2026-grid] [@gmrt-2026-synthesis]; the [bathymetry page](/en/bathymetry) sets out
exactly how much of the gulf floor has actually been sounded, and it is a small fraction.
Global multibeam and trackline databases record no systematic survey ever having been
run inside the gulf itself [@ncei-2026-multibeam]. That gap, more than any single
finding, is the reason this site exists: to state plainly what is known about the Golfo
Dulce, on what authority, and — just as importantly — what still is not.
